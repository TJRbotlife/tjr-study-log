## What we learned

He says execution is no longer the missing piece. A trade taken against the day's direction is, in his telling, either short-lived or simply a loss. Daily bias is the filter that decides which way the session is allowed to be traded.

The read starts on the weekly, then the daily. He treats the weekly as the longer direction and uses daily market structure for the day itself. A line chart is only a way to see that slope. On the S&P example he walks, the weekly is up, the daily has broken structure down, the four-hour is still up, and the hourly is mixed. He treats that disagreement as a reason to stay light on targets. Until the four-hour also turns down, he will not aim at distant downside objectives.

He ties the daily candle on the S&P to the New York session. Most of the day's range, he says, is made there, and the rest is chop. If the session does not travel with the daily bias, the trade is likely stopped. He reads that as the bias being wrong, not as a reason to force the other side. He also says he sat out that particular day because a public speaker was on the calendar.

The method he repeats is to imagine the daily, four-hour, and hourly as the trade being considered, mark the building blocks that agree with that bias, and only then scale down for a sweep, a break of structure, an order block, or a fair value gap. A four-hour or daily idea can still be entered on a five-minute or fifteen-minute confirmation. London, in his S&P example, is not where he wants that execution, even when the higher-frame story is already there.

When the daily has broken up, a lower-frame break down is a retrace into those building blocks, not a new bearish day. Equilibrium, a fair value gap, and an order block are the areas he waits for before looking for bullish confirmation on the hourly and below.

## What to remember

- Daily structure sets the session direction. A trade against it is the kind he expects to stall or fail.
- Weekly slope is context. Disagreement with the four-hour keeps distant targets off the table until that frame agrees.
- On the S&P, he locates the day's real move in the New York session.
- The same building blocks used for entries are how he confirms the bias, one frame at a time, down to the execution chart.
- A lower-frame break against the daily is a retrace into a zone, not a new bias.
