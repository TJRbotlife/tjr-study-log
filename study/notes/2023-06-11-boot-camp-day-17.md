## What we learned

Patience is the reason rushed entries happen. He says people lose trades they did not need, get excited, and press the button before any confirmation. The same habit shows up outside the charts. A result that looks quick will still take time, and he puts the horizon at a year, two years, or three, not a week and not a month.

He ties the urge to a short attention span and a hit of dopamine. He cites a gambling observation that the lift arrives when the bet is placed, and that the lift can be stronger than the feeling when the money arrives. Trading works the same way in his telling: the press of the button is the hit, and the mind is already spending a profit it has not earned. He wants the other side of that picture in view, the money that can be lost.

His practical answer is a written plan that is a step list. In his example the list runs through a liquidity sweep, a break of structure, a fair value gap, a reaction, and only then an entry. If the steps are missing, there is no trade. The point of the list is to make waiting ordinary, so patience is not a mood he has to summon. He also wants the session to open with a bias toward sitting out. The market has to offer a reason. Inventing a direction, forcing a bias the tape is not showing, or treating one piece of confluence as enough are the habits he is trying to drop.

Even a completed list can still mean no trade. News is his example. Waiting one or two more candles for volume and volatility is, in his view, a small cost next to the pressure of a job where the day can lose money. Goals can stay ambitious. He says they arrive sooner when they are not forced onto a one-month clock, and that each person's timeline differs. He names two years as his own path to consistent results.

The work stays individual. Friends running the same general idea will still read the tape differently, and he wants those sessions kept separate. He says the skill comes first and the money follows, and that his own day is often a single trade. The homework is a thirty-minute sit in a dark room, phone outside the door, eyes closed, attention on the breath, with no cut to ten or fifteen minutes. He says a shorter daily version, about ten minutes after a cold shower, is what he has used himself.

## What to remember

- The press of the button is the dopamine event he is warning about, separate from whether the trade later pays.
- A written step list is how he makes waiting automatic. Missing steps mean the session can stay flat.
- A finished checklist can still be a no-trade day when news, or a weak tape, argues for sitting out.
- Timelines differ. He treats a rushed month as slower than patient work over a longer span.
- The review and the decisions stay personal. Other people's bias is not part of the session.
