## What we learned

He recaps two trades and spends the lesson on how to choose when several charts look alive at once. The first was a pound/dollar long. The four hour was up and the hourly was down. His recent read is to find accumulation sitting under a strong push higher. London session lows had been swept, and a five-minute high had broken, which he says was still too small to count as prominent. He marked a fair value gap, waited for price to push into it and break one-minute structure, and entered there. The stop started under one low and was then moved under a deeper low, so a full fill of the gap would still be valid until the London sweep itself was taken. Targets sat on order blocks and other reaction areas above. Price stopped him out.

He treats that loss as something to study. What he would change is waiting for a close above a prominent five-minute high before getting in. He contrasts it with a pound/yen loss from two days earlier, which he calls an acceptable loss, and this one, which he says skipped that extra confirmation. In the lesson's own figures he reports about ten thousand dollars on that stop.

The second example is the S&P, built the same way: higher-time-frame direction, an accumulation or order block, then a lower-time-frame reaction. A five-minute break alone was not enough for him. He says August had been handing people a smart-money-style setup with only one or two of the usual checks, and those entries were getting stopped. After a sweep of liquidity and a one-minute break, he entered on a five-minute bullish close, stop under the lows. The first target hit and the rest stopped at break even. He reports the day down about a quarter of one percent, about five thousand dollars in this recap, because the pound stop had been widened when he moved it.

The teaching is what to do with two, three, or four setups. He had also been watching pound/yen for a long that never printed. When he mapped that idea against pound/dollar, the best reward he could see into a nearby reaction area was about one-to-one, and in one mapping closer to 0.7, while pound/dollar offered something nearer 1.5 to 2 on the first target. Same family of setup, so he took the better reward. A day that might hold two trades has to be sized for that ahead of time. The ten-thousand-dollar stop was, in his telling, about half of what he was willing to lose that day, so the first trade went on at half with a second idea still possible. The S&P partial then cut the day's loss. Taking only the loser, he says, is how a missed second setup turns into a revenge trade. The three comparisons he names are confluence, fit with the daily bias, and reward. If two of three ideas are clearly stronger, he splits the day's risk across those two. If the second idea never triggers, the first loss is still only part of the day's allowance, which in the talk is better than a full one, two, or three percent down. Full size on the pound alone, he says, would have been about twenty thousand dollars in this example.

## What to remember

- He called the pound/dollar loss a lesson because the five-minute high he used was not a prominent one.
- He treated an earlier pound/yen loss as acceptable, and this entry as one he would have waited longer on.
- The session in his figures ended down about a quarter of one percent after one full stop and one partial winner.
- Pound/yen was passed over because the mapped reward into the next reaction area was poor beside pound/dollar.
- He put the first trade on at about half his day's risk so a second idea could still be taken after a loss.
- A second setup that never appears still leaves the day inside that partial loss, which is the outcome he describes.
