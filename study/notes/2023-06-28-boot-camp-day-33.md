## What we learned

Part three covers the two remaining stacks on charts. He says the point is repetition of ideas already named, put in a sequence a person can see. Before the examples he notes that he stood aside on the index that day because a Federal Reserve chair was speaking, even though the chart printed a pattern he was willing to walk through. That pattern was a sweep of the lows, a fifteen-minute break upward, and an imbalance. He is careful that this one was a fair value gap entry, distinct from an order-block entry. He suggests scaling to a lower timeframe for the confirming break, and he says the stop location is still untaught. After the fill, the example moves toward older liquidity.

The rest of that half is the same sequence. Liquidity is taken, structure breaks, an imbalance is marked from candle to candle, price returns into it, and he watches the reaction, often toward prior highs or lows. One case is already with the larger move, and the gap is the return. Another shows a first reaction at a gap and a second at an order block. He treats a filled imbalance, with a structure break as confirmation, as something the viewer should now be able to see without a new concept. The assignment is about ten of these, with the reaction noted, and then ten of the equilibrium version.

Equilibrium, he says, is the same checklist with the halfway line drawn on it. When a retracement candle appears, he measures the swing and looks for the halfway mark to land on the gap or the block. A match is what he calls a high-confluence case. He treats the gap and the midpoint as the later locations: the first click was missed, or the person was away, and the return into discount is the later chance. In one example the whole consolidation is an order block, and a wick into the halfway line is the more precise touch. He counts those overlapping conditions out loud and says that is when fear of clicking should be lowest, because the same picture has been seen many times.

He ends with a limit on all of it. A sweep and a break on a small timeframe can still print when they disagree with the daily direction. He says that move is often short, and it can fade before a first target, because it is running against the larger direction he has in mind. The repetitions are there so the pictures feel familiar. The daily direction is the filter he says those pictures still need.
