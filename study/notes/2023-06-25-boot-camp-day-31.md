## What we learned

He treats this day as a return to the opening idea of the series. Effort and results travel together. Minimum work, he says, produces a minimum result. The standard he borrows from a business talk is that catching someone who is already ahead means doing several times the work that person is doing now, because that person did more while they were behind. His own early picture is long days of videos, on the order of eight hours, and a stack of day-trading books plus psychology books. He says the pace did not drop after the skill showed up. Getting better at the charts, in his telling, freed time to work harder at the next skill.

He turns that into a test. If the chart work, the reading, or the journal is producing no visible improvement, the dose goes up. A four-hour day carved out of phone and video time is the benchmark he passes along, and he says his own early days were longer. A story he repeats, from a player whose coaches would not play him, is to work until leaving that person out stops being available. The athlete example he uses treats the skill as physical gifts plus extra practice relative to teammates, repeated more often than the people around him. Saying a goal aloud, he says, only helps because the work follows the sentence. The sentence itself does not carry the result. People who think a stated goal is magic, in his account, are noticing the hours they put in after they said it.

He also says the lessons are about to move faster, and he names two responses when a concept arrives too quickly. One is to drop back to a simpler method with a weaker record. The other is to ask what extra study would make the concept clear, and then do that study. Thirty minutes, in his accounting, buys a thirty-minute result, and no study buys none. He says motivation is already fading for people who wanted the entertainment, and that discipline is what gets the repetition done on mornings when the desire is gone. Early progress is what makes the repetition sticky, the way the first visible gym results make someone return. A month into the series, he wants some sign that the work is changing the study: cleaner recognition, a better read of reward, entries that land more exactly, and lessons taken from mistakes. If none of that is there, he says the work is still too small, or the mistakes are going unused.

The close is about fear of trying, and about satisfaction. A dream that stays verbal, with no hours behind it, stays a dream. He wants pride in the progress so far, and he refuses satisfaction as a stopping point. His picture is his own public goals, which he says he will raise again after he reaches them. The people a student is copying, he says, are still trying to get better. The homework is to rewatch the first day and to check the short-term goals from the goal-setting lesson.

## What to remember

- He ties results to the size of the work, and he treats a flat study stretch as a sign to increase that work.
- A stated goal is the prompt. The hours after it are what he credits.
- When a lesson moves too fast, the response he wants is more study of that lesson.
- Discipline is the part that remains after a motivational day fades.
- The early evidence he names is cleaner recognition, a clearer read of reward, and fewer repeated mistakes, checked against the goals already written.
