## What we learned

He names three ways to choose a size. The one he uses is a set lot, sometimes inside a small range. The second is calculating a new lot for every trade from the account balance, a percent, and the stop distance. The third is risking the entire account, which he says he does not want anyone to do.

He demonstrates a calculator twice, once on a phone and once on a site that also includes the S&P. In the lesson's example the balance is one thousand dollars, the percent is one, and the stop is thirty pips. The site returns a figure he reads as 0.03 lots, which he describes as ten dollars at risk on that example. He does not carry the lot past two decimal places. The pip distance is not guessed. The platform's position tool shows it as the stop is dragged, and that number is what he types in.

The index is the case that changes the inputs. Stops there are often discussed in points, and he switches the tool so the calculator still receives a pip figure. Contract size is not assumed. He opens the symbol details and reads the contract-size line. On his platform that line says ten units per lot. He says many accounts use one hundred, and some firms use ten, so each new account has to be read again. Otherwise the lot the calculator prints is the wrong risk.

The set-size version starts from a stop he already expects. On the S&P, the lesson's example account is one hundred thousand dollars, and his usual stop band is about four to seven points, roughly four hundred pips at the tight end. He calculates the lot that risks about one percent at that tighter stop. In the example that lot is twenty-five. The same lot then risks more if the stop is wider: about two percent at roughly twice the distance, and about three percent at roughly three times, which he says rarely happens. That is why a set size is convenient for him, and also why it is pair-specific. A different market has a different typical stop because volume and volatility differ.

He then splits his own set size into a normal day, a half-size day when news or a holiday is likely to make conditions messy, and a doubled size when every bias lines up and there is no news. He says the doubled size is not for viewers, because it assumes a record they do not have yet. This note keeps those figures as his examples.

## What to remember

- Size in this lesson is either calculated from balance, percent, and stop distance, or held fixed from a typical stop. Risking the whole account is the option he throws out.
- The thousand-dollar, one-percent, thirty-pip case, and the 0.03-lot result, are the calculator demonstration. They are not a size to copy.
- Contract size is part of the input. He reads it from the symbol details because a lot is not the same number of units on every account.
- A fixed lot stays near the percent he chose only while the stop stays near the width he used to build it.
- The half-size and double-size split is how he talks about his own calendar and confidence. He tells viewers not to use the larger one.
