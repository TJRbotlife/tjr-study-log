## What we learned

He builds the lesson on that day's CPI release, after watching people try to trade before, during, and after the print. The calendar he demonstrates is Forex Factory. He clears the yellow items, which he treats as routine noise, and he keeps the gray items because they mark holidays and days when markets are shut. He then filters the board down to the currencies on the pairs he already trades, so the page shows only what can hit those markets. The clock on the site follows the viewer's own time zone.

A red folder means he waits for the number. He shows GBP/JPY and gold printing huge wicks at the CPI release and calls that tape something he will not trade. If a single red or orange item comes out and the market is calm fifteen or twenty minutes later, he may look, and he still calls the day lower probability. He says he would take less risk and would need the market to offer a clear reason. CPI, in his experience, always moves the market, so that exception does not apply.

Four releases sit in a separate category. He will not trade CPI, PPI, FOMC, or nonfarm payrolls at all. He walks the rest of that week as a calendar full of dollar releases and says a crowded red-folder day is a low-probability session even when it is not one of those four. On an ordinary Friday print that lands about thirty minutes after his cash open, he still wants price to develop before he considers anything. The whole week being crowded is itself a reason to stay patient.

Reading the number is forecast versus actual. The forecast is what the calendar expected. The actual is what printed, and that cell is blank until the release. The line he cares about is the usual effect: if the actual is greater than the forecast, the note calls that good for the currency. He spends the rest of the video on what that sentence does not mean. Good for the currency means good for the dollar, the DXY, and bad for markets priced against the dollar. In his telling that includes the S&P, gold, and pairs with the dollar on the quote side. The same day's CPI is his proof that the first reaction can lie. The actual came in below the forecast, the dollar dipped, then the dollar rallied and ate the dip. He treats price action as the higher authority, which is why he waits after the print.

The pair is a fraction. News that helps the currency on top of the slash pushes the pair up. News that hurts the currency on the bottom also pushes the pair up. Help for the bottom, or harm for the top, pushes the pair down. GBP news that is good for sterling is therefore a different reading on GBP/JPY than dollar news is on a pair quoted against the dollar. He wants that diagram written down, and he wants the calendar opened before the session so a release cannot arrive a few minutes after an entry.

## What to remember

- The calendar he uses separates noise, holidays, and the currencies attached to the pairs he trades.
- A red or orange release is a wait-and-watch event in his process. A huge wick after the print is a reason he stays out.
- CPI, PPI, FOMC, and nonfarm payrolls are the four he treats as sessions he skips entirely.
- "Good for the currency" means the currency in the release, which for dollar data is the dollar, not the S&P.
- Which side of the slash the currency sits on flips the pair's reading. The first spike can reverse, so the tape after the print outranks the headline.
