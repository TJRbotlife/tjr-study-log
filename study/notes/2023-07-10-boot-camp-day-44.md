## What we learned

Day 44 is about which markets to leave alone. He ties that choice to daily bias. A setup earns attention when the daily idea, the four-hour structure, the hourly structure, and the lower-time-frame confirmation all point the same way. His aligned example is an S&P that is bullish on the daily, has broken structure up on the four-hour, has swept liquidity, and has confirmed on the five-minute. The rejected case is a daily downtrend whose four-hour and hourly look up, with no nearby building block and no lower-time-frame confirmation. Everyone calling that pair bullish is, in his telling, arguing with the daily.

A chart with no direction is the other pass. Short-term trades inside that chop are possible, and he mentions marking hourly liquidity and using a very small break of structure to ride it back. He still wants the trade that matches the daily idea, and he says a four-hour break that then drops into a tangled lower-time-frame chart is itself a reason to stand aside. The markets he calls highest probability are the clean trends: higher highs and higher lows, or the bearish mirror, including a sweep that then continues. The chart he does not want is the one that fakes a break, sweeps, and reverses again. Stack a bearish daily, a bullish four-hour, and an hourly that does not read clearly, and he says that is not a moment to trade. News is in the same bucket for him. Sitting those days out is how he says he raises the odds.

If the pair in front of him fails that list, he wants another pair, or no trade that day when only one pair is on the desk. He says the traders he respects take the most days off, and he treats extra days in the market as extra chances to lose, because he says the market is built to beat the trader. A handful of trades that match the bias outranks a pile of mediocre ones taken for the feeling of being in. The checklist he repeats is where the day wants to go, where the four-hour wants to go, where the hourly wants to go, and only then a lower-time-frame execution inside the daily idea. A one-minute or five-minute break can be nothing but the fifteen-minute retrace. A fifteen-minute break can be the hourly retrace. Higher time frames hold more weight, so a small break does not get to rewrite the day.

The later stretch of the video leaves the chart and argues for long hours of study. He says his own mornings ran about six to eight hours, and he treats light effort as the path to ordinary results. He then brings the point back to a loss: the chart history already shows where price went, so the review is to see what the market did and what the trader took instead. He calls that the backtest that matters, paired with written trade reviews, because writing the reason down is what makes the next copy of the same situation recognizable.

## What to remember

- A usable setup, in this lesson, is daily, four-hour, hourly, and lower-time-frame confirmation all in the same direction. A small break does not replace the higher time frame.
- Chop, crossed time frames, and news are the conditions he passes on. Another pair is the first alternative, and a day with no trade is the second.
- He treats extra sessions as extra chances to lose, so the fewer, aligned trades are the ones he keeps.
- The urge to be in a trade is the thing this lesson is trying to interrupt. The market has to satisfy the whole list.
- A loss is reviewed on the chart that already printed, and the written note is how he expects the same situation to be recognized later.
