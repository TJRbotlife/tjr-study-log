## What we learned

He calls the written plan the page that makes a later strategy hard to violate. It is a mock-up on paper, answered in advance and kept in front of the screen. Risk is the first box. The day's loss is known before the session, so the loss is a number that was already accepted. On size, he separates a calculated lot from a few preset lot sizes. The calculator takes the balance, the percent, and the stop distance and returns a size. He uses presets himself, sorted into higher, ordinary, and lower risk, because calculating every time is slow for him and he has learned to estimate from how the stop looks. He tells newer people to calculate, because a preset lot can hide the real risk. He also fixes the count. His own day is one trade, sometimes two, and when it is two he cuts each so the pair still equals one day's risk. The sentence he wants on a beginner's page names a fixed daily amount and one trade. The amount in that worksheet example is one hundred dollars.

The second box is time. He wants a session chosen the way a job has hours, matched to sleep and to work. His own window is the New York open, and he wants to be in a trade within about the first hour and a half. If nothing qualifies, he is done looking. An open trade can remain. The third box is news. He separates the high-impact releases he names, including an inflation print, employment, a central-bank decision, and a producer-price print, from ordinary calendar items that only color a bias. A student, he says, can skip news entirely and protect the early win rate that way. He also sketches a personal filter the viewer has to set: if a release spikes the market by about five points inside five minutes, the page already says whether that day is off.

The fourth box is the market, and he wants one. He describes an index, a metal, and crypto in the same week as separate marketplaces with separate behavior. His own history is the illustration. He spent years on one yen cross before the index, gold, and other pairs were added, and the adding came after that one market was working. While he was still learning, two markets at once was the version he says failed him. The last box is confluence. He says it is still hard to finish in writing, because the combined execution lessons are where the full list gets spelled out. The method he gives now is to test one pairing, such as a sweep plus a structure break, for a couple of weeks, write the result, and journal how it felt. He often wants an order block or a gap unless the sweep and the break already offer the reward he is after. For someone in the viewer's seat he would require the larger trend, a sweep, and a structure break all pointing the same way.

His spoken checklist that day, on an index that was bullish on the daily, was buys only, the New York open only, that index only, and only after the risk number was known. A missing box ends the session. A holiday that closes that market ends it too, and he stays off the other markets. He says the page is what makes the click mechanical, so a hunch has nothing to act on. The homework is to write those answers down.

## What to remember

- The plan he wants on paper has the day's risk, the session, the single market, and the confluences.
- A preset size and a calculated size are different tools. He uses presets. He points newer study at the calculator so the risk stays visible.
- His session example is the New York open. If nothing qualifies inside the opening window, the search is over.
- High-impact news is a decision written before the day. Skipping those releases is the conservative version he offers while someone is still learning.
- One market is the study unit in this lesson. He added other markets only after a long stretch on the first.
- He treats a missing box on the page as a finished session.
