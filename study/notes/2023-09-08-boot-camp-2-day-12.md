## What we learned

The session is one long, on pound/dollar, and a talk about where a target belongs. Three targets were hit and the rest of the position stopped at break even. He says the day had no news. The daily was still pointing down. On a smaller scale he saw prominent highs broken to the upside, with orders accumulated in the zone that caused the break, plus a sweep to the downside as extra confluence. Scaled in, the five minute showed the same story: a tap of that order block, a sweep of the leg that had caused the push, a miniature downtrend broken, and then a one-minute close above a prominent high. That close was the entry. The stop sat under the accumulation that had caused the move up.

The targets are the tip he wants pulled out of the chart. The first was a high. The second sat above a high that had already been swept and then sold off. He says the orders were filled above that swept high, so the swept wick itself is the wrong place to expect the next reaction. A third target sat on another liquidity area, with a fourth and a fifth drawn up at higher-time-frame highs that the remainder never reached. The rest stopped at break even after the three that hit.

He places those levels where he expects price to react. Fibonacci targets, and other measured levels with no chart story, are what he sees other people use and what he leaves alone here. Several targets exist because he does not know which confluence will be the one that turns price: the swept high, something above it, a higher-time-frame order block, or a lower-time-frame one. The building blocks from the first boot camp — fair value gaps and liquidity among them — are what he uses. A low-time-frame entry can be aiming up while higher-time-frame blocks still cause swings along the way. If the first of those reactions is never booked, and the stop is never moved to break even, a correct directional read can still hand the gain back when price returns to sweep the entry lows and then continue. In the example, each level that price blew through had already taken profit with the stop at break even, and the eventual reaction came after the third.

He describes liquidity, in this talk, as the highs and lows already sitting on the chart. Marking them does not tell him which one will be swept with a break of structure. A one-minute break can bounce and keep going, so he would rather have the staged profits than wait for that break to become the exit. The same point is how he talks about a holding that is paying: book some while it is being given, because the turn is not announced. He frames a day already ahead as a day worth protecting from giving the gain back.

## What to remember

- The example was a single pound/dollar long that booked three targets and then stopped the rest at break even, on a day he said had no news.
- The second target sat above a swept high, on the prices where he said the sell orders had been filled.
- He places targets on chart areas where he expects a reaction, using the building blocks from the first series.
- Several possible turn points are why the exit is staged across more than one level.
- A lower-time-frame entry can be right about direction and still round-trip if those first reactions are never booked.
- Liquidity, in this talk, is the highs and lows already on the chart, and he does not claim to know which one gets swept next.
