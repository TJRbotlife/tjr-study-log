## What we learned

He opens by saying the recap should be about the trade itself. A larger account, and a broker change he says is coming, will make dollar wins and dollar losses look extreme. He does not want a viewer to treat an extreme figure as a result they can copy in a couple of days, or as proof he cannot trade. He leaves out other trades from the day, scalps on the index and on gold, because he does not want that style held up as the example.

The trade he does walk through is a pound-yen short in his usual session-high sequence. He passed on an earlier break because U.S. news was due, and he wanted the London highs taken first. They were. Structure broke down, he entered on the candle close, and the stop sat about four pips above those highs. The release ran price roughly six more pips, stopped the trade, and then price went the direction he had wanted. He says the cleaner choice was to wait until that high-impact U.S. print, including a policy appearance, was out. Because of the news he cut the trade to a quarter of one percent. On his own book that was a heavy cut. He describes his usual forex size as far larger, and his usual loss tolerance as on the order of twenty thousand dollars, so this one felt like repetition to him.

The room still treated the stop as a crisis. His classroom example is a thousand-dollar account: a quarter of one percent is about two dollars and fifty cents, and one percent is about ten dollars. He says a strong reaction at that scale means the person risked more than they understood, or they did not know the risk they had put on. Under a one-percent trade, his own feeling is that he is mostly getting repetitions. He also says a strong month can carry later losing months, and he points at an early month that year of about forty percent with no losing trade as that kind of month. The stance he wants, even before someone is profitable, is that a single loss does not have to be won back before the day is allowed to end. If the person is not yet profitable, he says that stance belongs on a practice account.

He lists ways people pin the risk down: one fixed size for a pair, a calculated risk on each trade, or a rough loss budget with a few preset sizes. The question under all of them is whether living costs are already covered, and whether the money in a live account can actually be lost. Emotional pain on a small loss, or while the trade is only in drawdown, is his sign that the money is still needed. From there he says the story people tell themselves, that a strategy will work quickly and that a loss has to be made back in the same market, is where the session becomes gambling and revenge. His stated preference is a practice account until the results are already there, or no wager at all.

## What to remember

- He wants the day's lesson attached to the trade, and dollar extremes are easy to misread on a larger account.
- This stop-out, in his telling, came from being in the short before the U.S. release.
- His thousand-dollar illustration is there to show how small a quarter percent is, and how loud the reaction still was.
- A practice account is where he puts someone who is not yet profitable.
- Living costs, and money that cannot be lost, sit under the size question in this lesson.
- The loop of losing and needing the same market to pay it back is what he calls the turn into gambling.
