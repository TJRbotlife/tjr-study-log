## What we learned

He recaps one loss and one win, and the win is the trade he trusts less. The loss was an S&P long. The direction was the one he wanted. The entry, he says, was early: price still wanted about three points lower. On the daily a downtrend had broken and price had returned to the area. A smaller downtrend then broke as well. He was looking for accumulation that could start a leg up, with liquidity resting underneath. Scaled in, liquidity was swept and structure broke up. He passed on that first break. He wanted a fair value gap filled and a reaction. The fill ran a little deep, one up candle could not close back above, a new high printed, and then a close above that high was the one-minute break he entered, stop underneath. Price stopped him, traded into a fifteen-minute accumulation that sat under the earlier large leg up, then broke structure, filled a gap, and rallied through the path he had marked.

He is at peace with that stop. The day before, a pound/yen entry without a third confirmation had bothered him. This one had the sweep, the break, a reaction he judged incomplete, the gap, and a second break before he got in. In the example the stop was about 2.7 points. The question he sits with is whether the whole accumulation zone was the higher-confluence level, and the sweep alone was the thinner one. Knowing the direction and missing the execution depth is, in his words, not a day to be hard on yourself about.

The win was pound/dollar, and he calls the execution sloppy even after it paid. A leg down before a large extension had broken a range and then returned to a consolidation. On the fifteen minute that leg was the order block. Price chopped through a revisit, then barely closed above a high. He looked for longs off an order block built when London lows were swept just after New York opened. On the one minute, a fair value gap fill and a take of the lows were not enough. What he actually took was a minor break of structure, with the stop about four pips under that sweep, on the idea that a return under the sweep would invalidate the long. The version he prefers, looking back, is waiting through the chop for the consolidation to break, a retest, and a five-minute bullish reaction, with the stop under the leg that filled the orders, a pip or two beyond it. That later entry would have been about a one-to-one at the first target. The first target on the trade he did take was hit, and he says it covered the S&P loss, in part because he risks less on the S&P than on the forex pairs and cannot really say why.

He sat in drawdown on that long for roughly an hour and a half before the extension. Logging off and skipping it, he says, would have left him equally satisfied with the day. The four hour on pound/dollar was still in a downtrend. The long was a bet on a fifteen-minute break of prominent highs acting as a retracement up into the accumulation that had caused the original leg down. It is not a trade he says he would want as a habit. The lower entry may have improved the reward, and the cleaner spot was the later breakout retest, the revisit of the gap and the accumulation, and the reaction off that candle. He was more satisfied with the losing S&P read than with the winning pound trade, because the hard part he names is execution and the stop, while the daily direction is the part that has to be right if the executions are going to win some and lose some.

He closes on the calendar of that week. CPI was next, PPI the day after, and Friday carried prints about an hour before the open, fifteen minutes before the open, and thirty minutes into the session. He says he might leave the rest of that week alone and still watch. A heavier news week is how he explains a softer stretch after three stronger ones, and he ties the stronger stretch to being back on a routine after travel. The win still counts, in the talk, as something to learn from because the entry was the weak part.
