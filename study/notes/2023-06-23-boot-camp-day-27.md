## What we learned

Day 27 is the other side of the confidence line from the overconfidence talk. He says a new student should want losses, because that is how the loss lesson says the skill grows. Wanting losses belongs, in his account, on a practice account. Real money makes that wish expensive. On practice money the sting is pride, and he points anyone who freezes there back to the lesson on taking a loss.

He is explicit that a short green stretch on practice is the trap he fell into himself. Two weeks of gains, he says, is the version people treat as permission. The bar he states for even considering live money is longer: about three months of gains, on the order of ten percent, from one trade a day at about one percent of the account. Passing that bar, he says, still leaves the word "profitable" unused. His definition is money taken from the market on a consistent basis. Practice results have rehearsed the click. He puts the chance of losing the first live account very high, around nine in ten, because the balance goes to the head and the old habits of extra size and extra trades return. The repair he names is a return to the practice account and a fresh look at why the risk rules existed.

Fear, he says, is the subconscious attachment to money. People decide a sum is expendable and discover the attachment after it is gone. He is rough on funding an account with money that would hurt, and he uses a thin net worth against a large share of it as the picture. His illustration for rehearsing real wins and real losses is a very small live balance, in the example a hundred-dollar account and a one-dollar loss, small enough that a win or a loss does not deserve excitement or grief. After another multi-month stretch of gains, he talks about a funded account or one's own capital, depending on what can actually be lost. He treats that stack of repetitions as the thing that leaves little room for doubt.

He also says psychology has no borrowed fix. Different people stall on greed, on overconfidence, or on fear of the click, and the conflict is with oneself. He cites the common claim that psychology is most of the work. The feeling he wants, once a written plan exists, is boredom: one decision, a small predefined risk, the same steps. He says the fear of clicking is often the residue of arriving each day with a new exciting method and losing. A step-by-step plan that has been seen to work, and that the student has repeated, is what he thinks should make the click ordinary. The boot camp, he says, can skip months of small mistakes, and it still leaves market feel to be earned afterward.

## What to remember

- Fear, here, is the freeze on the click. Overconfidence was the inflated click. He wants the band between them.
- His definition of profitable is a consistent take from the live market. Practice results are rehearsal.
- He expects the first contact with real money to revive old size and frequency habits, and he sends that failure back to practice.
- Attachment to the balance is the source he gives for fear. Money that would hurt is outside the account he is describing.
- Once a plan exists, the feeling he wants from the work is boredom and repetition.
